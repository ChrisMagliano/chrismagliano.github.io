  <section class="phd-section" id="research">
    <div class="phd-section-head"><span class="phd-kicker">SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH</span><h2>Exploring worlds beyond our Solar System.</h2></div>
    <p class="phd-section-intro">The main goal of my PhD was to contribute to the scientific preparation of <strong>ESA's PLATO mission</strong>, designed to discover and characterize exoplanets, including Earth-like worlds around Sun-like stars. I studied the planetary populations and observational challenges that make these discoveries possible.</p>
    <div class="phd-story-grid">
      <article class="phd-story"><span class="phd-story-label">PLANETARY POPULATIONS</span><h3>The mystery of Hot Neptunes</h3><p>Why are Neptune-sized planets so uncommon in extremely close orbits around their stars? I investigated the so-called <em>Hot Neptune Desert</em> through both candidate validation and theoretical modeling. In one <a href="https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/519/1/1562/6840254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">study</a>, our team statistically validated <strong>TOI-277 b and TOI-1288 b</strong> and identified additional promising targets. A subsequent study explored the desert in a new parameter space based on stellar properties.</p></article>
      <article class="phd-story"><span class="phd-story-label">ANALYTICAL MODELING</span><h3>When a planet transits only once</h3><p>Planetary orbital periods are usually inferred from repeated transits. But what if only a single transit is observed? I developed and tested an <a href="https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/528/2/2851/7582001" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"> analytical approach</a> for estimating orbital periods from mono-transit events, using TESS observations as prior knowledge for potential future PLATO targets.</p></article>
      <article class="phd-story"><span class="phd-story-label">ASTROBIOLOGY</span><h3>Could photosynthesis work on other worlds?</h3><p>I also explored the theoretical efficiency of oxygenic photosynthesis on Earth-like planets in the habitable zone. <a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022cosp...44..582M/abstract" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">This work</a> connected stellar radiation, planetary environments, and the physical conditions that might support life, and was presented at the National Congress of Planetary Sciences and the COSPAR Scientific Assembly.</p></article>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="phd-section phd-section-tint" id="citizen-science">
    <div class="phd-section-head"><span class="phd-kicker">LARGE-SCALE ASTRONOMICAL DATA</span><h2>Finding the signal in the noise.</h2></div>
    <p>As part of <strong>NASA's Planet Patrol</strong> citizen-science project, I joined the scientific core team investigating exoplanet candidates identified by the <strong>TESS</strong> space telescope. The project brought researchers and volunteers together to distinguish genuine planetary signals from astrophysical or instrumental false positives.</p>
    <p>I led the first-author study <em>The TESS Triple-9 Catalog II</em>, in which we systematically examined <strong>999 exoplanet candidates</strong> using the Python-based DAVE validation pipeline and the contributions of citizen scientists. I also took part in regular project meetings, discussing ongoing analyses and helping volunteers understand how to vet planetary signals.</p>
    <p>Beyond this project, I contributed to TESS collaboration studies of terrestrial planets, multi-planet systems, and young planetary systems. These experiences deepened my interest in reproducible analysis, statistical validation, and computational workflows.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="phd-section" id="engineering">
    <div class="phd-section-head"><span class="phd-kicker">SPACE ENGINEERING</span><h2>From planetary science to telescope hardware.</h2></div>
    <p>My PhD also took me into an industrial research environment at <strong>Leonardo S.p.A.</strong>, where I spent two research periods working on experimental tests of PLATO's telescope optical units. The tests investigated optical performance under mechanical, thermal, and electrical stresses, including changes in the <em>Point Spread Function</em> under different environmental conditions.</p>
    <p>I developed <strong>Python tools to automate and analyze experimental test data</strong> and studied the performance of the assembly, integration, and testing processes. Results from this work contributed to a presentation at the <strong>SPIE conference in Japan</strong> on PLATO telescope optical testing in ambient and cryo-vacuum environments.</p>
    <aside class="phd-aside"><strong>At the telescope</strong><p>I also participated in observational activities at the Asiago Observatory, remotely operating the 1.82 m Copernico telescope under expert supervision to observe five dynamically new comets in connection with ESA's Comet Interceptor project.</p></aside>
  </section>

  <section class="phd-section phd-section-tint" id="community">
    <div class="phd-section-head"><span class="phd-kicker">COLLABORATION & COMMUNICATION</span><h2>Science is a collective endeavor.</h2></div>
    <p>Research gave me the chance to exchange ideas with scientists across institutions and disciplines. I was invited to present my work on Hot Neptunes in an online seminar at the <strong>Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)</strong>, and shared findings at COSPAR, Italian planetary-science meetings and PLATO science meetings.</p>
    <p>I also developed and led an observing proposal for the <strong>James Webb Space Telescope</strong>, aimed at characterizing the atmosphere of TOI-1288 b. Although the proposal was not selected, the process offered valuable experience in formulating a scientific case and designing an observational program.</p>
